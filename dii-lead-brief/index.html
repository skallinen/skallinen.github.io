Making the outcomes stick

A vague list produces nothing, so every line has to carry the same few facts.

Each Kill List item, four facts: the task in concrete verbs, in the order a person does it. Who does it today, a role or a name. How often, per sprint, per week, per release. Roughly how long, a range is fine. Plus the one that is easy to skip and worth the minute: how they would know the output was good enough to use. If the group cannot say how they would check it, say so plainly.

If they are stuck on whether something belongs on the Kill List, ask: “If we threw the work away and had it made again from scratch, would this part have to survive?” What has to survive stays with you, and the rest is a candidate to hand over.

The Wish List item, one line: who does what, how often, starting roughly when.

What makes a Wish List line good: two shapes both count. Better work the freed time now allows, something the group can only do together, like getting properly agreed on something or checking that what was agreed is what everyone thinks was agreed. Or new work the change itself creates, like reviewing output that arrives faster than anyone can check it, writing down what a system is meant to do so it survives being regenerated, or bringing somebody into a system nobody wrote by hand. If the line is the same execution work, just more of it or faster, nudge once with “what would that time let us finally sort out together?” or “what will we have to start doing that nobody does today?”

The experiment, four facts: what will be tried in one sentence, present tense. Who owns it, one name, not a team. When they have blocked time, a day and an hour. What they will show at the AI Hub in a month.

The test. Could someone who was not in your group pick up the sheet and start on Monday. If no, it is not finished. Apply it to every line before you hand the paper over.

A bad Kill List item, invented and generic, use it as a shape:

“Documentation. It takes ages and nobody reads it.”

The same thing, good:

“Writing the release notes at the end of each sprint. Done by whoever closes the sprint, every two weeks, about 90 minutes. We would check it by comparing against the tickets that actually merged.”

Somebody could start the second one on Monday, and the last sentence is the part that makes it usable.

Why the Wish List is there

Ask only what people would hand to AI and one sum runs silently around the table: if everyone drops half their work, half of us are surplus. Nobody says it out loud, and it quietly shapes every answer you get. The Wish List gives the freed time somewhere to go, which is what makes the first half safe to answer honestly. A group that knows the Wish List is coming gives you a much better Kill List, so name both lists in your opening minute.

Why we are doing this at all

Sami argues it from the stage before he hands over. Making code is getting cheap, and nobody knows how far that goes. If it holds, the constraint does not vanish. By Goldratt’s rule it moves, and the belief is that it moves onto the shared understanding of what is worth building and why. That understanding was always the hard part, and it used to get built on the way to something else, because you could not write the code without working out what the thing should do. Take the expensive step away and the thinking stops happening by itself. Peter Naur named the thing at stake back in 1985: what a team really holds is a theory of the program, and the code is only its residue. What has changed is the economics, so the question becomes what we carry the theory in. Whatever we carry it in is a map, and a map is useful only because it leaves things out, so deciding what it leaves out is the work. What remains is the work that was always hardest and was rarely anyone’s job.

His slide lists what always hard actually means. Every line is a week that went somewhere, and it is the most useful thing to have in your head for your 45 minutes:

  1. Agreeing, in the first place.
  2. Checking that what we agreed is what everyone thinks we agreed.
  3. Working out what other people already understand.
  4. Noticing who is not in the room, and which knowledge left with them.
  5. Holding a shared model of what the thing actually does.
  6. Keeping that model alive as people leave, join and hand over.

None of them is a typing problem. Groups reach for typing first: boilerplate, test data, ticket writing, release notes. Those are real and they belong on the list. If your group produces only those, push once past typing with “when did we last lose a week to a misunderstanding?” The six are raw material for the second shape of Wish List line, the one a group can only do together. What makes a Wish List line good, earlier in this half, is what one of them looks like once it is written down and owned.

Hold the six lightly. They are one person’s framing, arrived at from his own background, and they are where the argument came from rather than a list to comply with. Your group should work from what they have actually lived this year, and your job is to keep their items meaningful and concrete. Walking them onto these six is no part of it. The failure to watch for is a group producing something generic or dutiful, and the fix is the same one you use all afternoon: ask for the last time it actually happened to them.

“That list is where the idea came from. It is not what we have to produce. Tell me what has really cost us time this year, and we will work from that.”

If someone asks you why you are all doing this, say:

“Making things got cheap. Agreeing what to make did not. We are looking for the parts of our week worth handing over, and for what we would do with the time that frees up.”
